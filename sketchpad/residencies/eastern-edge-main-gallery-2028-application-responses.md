## Artist Bio (approx. 150 words) *

Adam Simms is a media artist from Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland), based in Tiohtià:ke (Montreal). Using cameras, data, and microelectronics, he creates works that treat environment and cultural memory as interconnected material.

For over a decade his work has centred on Pinchard's Island, the outport his grandmother resettled from in the 1960s. Through his family's history, he asks which aspects of intangible culture are portable and which are rooted in place.

Cloudberry, Cabin, and Driftwood used solar cameras, real-time data, and sensors to challenge remote presence, collapse distance, and transmit a disappearing outport vernacular to unexpected locations.

His work has appeared in Riddle Fence and Ciel Variable, and been exhibited at the Leonard & Bina Ellen Art Gallery and Occurrence. He holds a BFA in Design Art and an MFA in Studio Arts from Concordia University, where he has also taught as an Artist-in-Residence.

## Artist Statement outlining an overview of your practice/interests (max. 500 words) *

*(473 words, limit 500)*

Pinchard's Island is a treeless outport off the northeast coast of Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland), reachable only when the ocean permits. My grandmother was born and raised there. In the 1960s, residents uprooted their homes and floated them across the bay until the community was reduced to a few gravestones. Years later, my extended family began going back and built an off-grid cabin. During those returns, we made soup, flew kites, told stories, and cooked toutons over an open fire. My family are collaborators in the work that came out of those returns, not subjects, with authority over their own voice and representation.

I am from Newfoundland, and I have lived away for half my life. Belonging remains partial, both from the place and outside it. I am unsettled, and I am resettled. That distance is the material of my work: a longing to be present in places that history has tried to erase, and to understand which parts of intangible culture are portable and which are rooted in place.

I work with photography, video, sculpture, and microelectronics. For over a decade the practice has returned to Pinchard's Island through machines that look and objects that hold: solar cameras, live data, sensors, and forms built to carry landscape and vernacular material between the island and wherever I am living. *Cloudberry* fixed a solar camera to our cabin and held one window for over a year until the light failed. *Cabin* rebuilt a Newfoundland saltbox hearth in a Tiohtià:ke (Montreal) high-rise studio, with that feed as its window. *Driftwood* was a piece of wood from the Atlantic that rose and fell on live buoy data. *Light House* held a house in light on the coastline, an afterlife for resettled homes. In each of them presence is partial, remote, and permitted to fail.

I keep asking the camera to do something it cannot: hold a place that is gone. The question underneath those works was how to represent a place that no longer exists. A more urgent one has since taken over. What is lost as the last generation of Pinchard's Islanders fades? My uncle's names for coves that are on no map, the way my grandmother bottles beets, the words I have heard nowhere else.

That carrying is never neutral. Pinchard's Island is Ktaqmkuk, and any apparatus that transmits or invents presence on resettled, colonized ground is not innocent of the erasure that made the outport possible. I hold that in the work rather than resolve it. My practice now moves in two directions at once: image systems that keep asking what a machine can hold of a place after the tether breaks, and work meant to remain in place, which asks how much can be held, and what is lost, when an object outlasts its people and the knowledge they carry.

## Exhibition Proposal clearly outlining what the work is, and how it will be presented (max. 500 words) *

*(471 words, limit 500)*

In August 2017 I built Cloudberry, a solar-powered camera system mounted on my family's cabin on Pinchard's Island, an outport off Newfoundland's northeast coast where my grandmother Doris was born. The camera worked alone, photographing the same north-facing window once an hour, unattended from that August into the following March, until the cold and the short days drew more power than the sun could return and it went dark.

*Dreamberry* is that camera still dreaming. The apparatus is gone, but a custom model continues generating the window it can no longer photograph, trained on the original Cloudberry photographs and held to that same frame. The weather is the truest thing in the work: a live feed from the island shapes the image, so a new view is dreamed each time the weather turns, and the window morphs from one dream into the next rather than resting on a fixed image. Everything in the frame is imagined except the weather moving through it.

Every dreamed view is kept and fed back into training, so the model increasingly dreams from its own dreams. Where the AI industry trains on everything and grows more fluent, this model trains on itself and grows less certain: the frame holds, but the place inside it drifts, and the island the camera saw will eventually be unrecoverable from the machine that replaced it. How long that takes is unknown.

Cloudberry first found a room in *Cabin*, where I built a hearth around the camera's live feed. For Dreamberry, the gallery does the opposite: the view hangs on a bare wall. Two portrait LED panels sit at sill height with a narrow gap between them, echoing the two panes of the original window. Frosted plexi covers the panels, and light falls on the wall around them, so the audience reads light rather than pixels. The room is dim and quiet. Whoever pauses watches the weather turn; whoever passes through sees a view that appears to stand still.

Across from that window hangs the archive the model dreams from: a grid of Cloudberry prints, and on two offset clotheslines fabric prints that float and turn and are hard to hold still; every frame is the same window in a different hour, the light the only difference. Framed Pinchard's Island photographs and a portrait of Doris hang as lineage rather than as a retrospective, with a large photograph of the cabin window facing the LED diptych at the same sill height. Everything photographed stays on the wall; everything in the light is dreamed.

The Dreamberry AI model is trained and starting to dream images. What is left to build is its window: I make the LED panels and plexi during an accepted residency at Arteles in Finland in February 2027, from stackable modules that travel as luggage. Printing and hang tests run through 2027; the work would be ready for any exhibition slot in 2028 and 2029.
