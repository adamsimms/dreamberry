## 1. Project description (cap 400)

*Intentions, questions explored, state of progress, chosen period, stages of exploration or production.*

### Cut version (344 words, send this to translation)

Cloudberry was a solar-powered camera I built on Pinchard's Island, a Newfoundland outport emptied by resettlement. It photographed the view from our family's off-grid cabin window every hour until winter starved it of power. Dreamberry is that camera still dreaming: an image model trained only on its photographs, steered by the island's live weather. The weather is true. The rocks are memory and invention. Unlike models trained on everything they can find, it knows one view, and each dream is fed back into its training, so it slowly forgets the island. A resettled place is lost the same way, once no one is left to remember it.

A first version already dreams on a screen. The project is a window of light: two portrait LED panels, 30 by 50 in each, echoing the cabin window's two panes, behind frosted acrylic. I will arrive with the bare LED panels running, ready to build their housing with the specialist.

The residency answers a question I cannot answer in a studio: how does this window meet a space? At what darkness and distance does the light stop reading as pixels and start reading as a place? Does it read better mounted on a wall, or hung free in the dark? What do haze, and the island's wind at the edge of hearing, add or take away? Each is asked of a live work: a new dream arrives at least hourly, so the window changes as I test it.

Week one is fabrication and installation in the dark. Week two is adjustment, starting with the low-brightness rendering in software. Near the end, a few people from Québec City say what they see before I explain, followed by an open studio and artist talk.

This needs a black box, its lighting and sound, the workshops in the building, and a specialist who knows how light behaves when asked to almost disappear. By the end, it will have stood in a room, in front of its first audience.

First choice: 7 June to 2 July 2027, in the studio d'Essai.

### Long version (reference only, do not send)

Cloudberry was a solar camera I built on my family's cabin on Pinchard's Island, an outport off the northeast coast of Newfoundland emptied by resettlement in the 1960s. From 2017 it photographed the same window once an hour, until the cold and the short days drew more power than the sun could return and it went dark. It never invented a frame.

Dreamberry is that camera still dreaming. An image model trained only on Cloudberry's photographs generates the view it can no longer take, steered by live weather at the island's coordinates. Most image models take in everything they can reach and grow more fluent. This one knows a single view of one island, and each of its dreams is fed back into its training, so it grows less certain and loses what my family gave it in the open, the way the island reached me: as retellings of retellings. The weather is true at the moment you stand in front of it. The rocks are memory and invention.

A first version already dreams on a screen. This winter, in Montréal and during a residency at Arteles in Finland, I am training the model on the archive and building a small prototype window, 64 by 48 cm, with LED modules at three pixel pitches behind frosted acrylic, to learn how little resolution can still hold a place. In spring I wire and test the full-size set of modules in Montréal and draft a sound from the same weather data. What does not exist yet is the system that runs Dreamberry at full size, and that is what I would build at Recto-Verso.

The system has three parts, built with the specialist and tested in the dark. The display: two portrait LED panels, about 30 by 50 in each, side by side with a narrow gap like the two panes of the cabin window, behind frost. The live chain: from the island's weather, to the model, to the panes. And a sound engine driven by the same weather. The stay is a sensory and technological experiment around four questions.

Can the light stay dim? The window lives at the bottom of an LED panel's brightness range, where panels band, flicker and lose colour. With the specialist I want to build the dimming and the slow fades between dreams so the window glows like weather through glass rather than like a screen.

Can it hold, live, for two weeks? A new dream arrives each time the island's weather changes, at least once an hour, and each one is fed back into the model. Over the stay it retells the island more than three hundred times, so someone who visits on the first day and again on the last will not see the same island. The chain has to survive that run, through dropped networks, a stalled model and a weather feed gone quiet, and the window has to know what to do when it does: hold, go dark, or play from files.

Should the weather be heard? I arrive with a draft sound built from the same live data, wind over the island, and want to turn it into an engine that plays at the edge of hearing on the room's speakers. I want to know whether sound brings the place nearer or only makes it louder.

What does the room do to it? Once the system runs, I test it where only a black box allows: at a distance, through haze and tulle, against measured spill, and for someone walking in from a lit corridor.

Week one is the build with the specialist: framing and mounting the panels, power, the controller and the low-brightness rendering, then first sittings in full dark. Week two is the live chain under load, the sound engine and the room tests. Near the end of the second week I invite a few people from Québec City to sit with the window and say what they see before I say what it is. On the last day I open the studio and hold a conversation on machines, forgetting and resettlement.

I write the model and the software, and I can wire LED modules on a bench in Montréal. What I cannot do alone is make that light behave at full size in the last few percent before dark, keep the whole chain running without a break, or test it in a dark room this size. The light will not hold everything, and I want to stay with what it cannot hold rather than correct it. The work does not need to be finished when the two weeks end. It needs to have stood in a room.

**Period:** first choice 7 June to 2 July 2027 (studio d'Essai). Also possible: 17 May to 5 June 2027 (salle Multi), or 21 June to 1 July 2027 (salle Multi).

## 4. Artistic approach (cap 250)

Pinchard's Island is a treeless outport off the northeast coast of Ktaqmkuk (Newfoundland). My grandmother was born and raised there. In the 1960s a government resettlement program emptied the island: residents floated their houses across the bay until only gravestones remained. Years later my extended family began going back and built an off-grid cabin. My practice is rooted in those returns, often with my family as collaborators.

Distance is the material of my work. I am from Newfoundland, and I have lived away for half my life. I am unsettled, and I am resettled. I long to be present in places that history has tried to erase, and to understand which parts of intangible culture are portable and which are rooted in place.

I build my own machines, hardware and code, alongside photography, video, and sculpture. Cloudberry held our cabin's window until the light failed. Cabin rebuilt a Newfoundland saltbox hearth in a Montreal studio, with Cloudberry's live feed as its window. Driftwood rose and fell on live buoy data. Light House glowed at the edge of the shore. Those machines recorded. Dreamberry, the work I am making now, is the first that invents. In each, presence is partial, remote, and permitted to fail.
